# CV & Resume Writer

This is the distilled consensus of the **20 most-viewed English videos on writing a CV or resume uploaded 25 Apr - 24 Oct 2026**, capped at 2 per channel. 18 of the 20 transcripts were available: 16 channels, about 57,000 words. Sources: `references/sources.md`. Full theme-by-video matrix: `references/consensus-tally.md`. Layouts, worked examples and the review scorecard: `references/templates-and-examples.md`.

Every principle carries a weight, shown as **(videos / 18 · channels / 16)**. Channels count more than videos, because two videos from one creator are one opinion, not two.

- **Core**: ≥8 of 16 channels and ≥9 of 18 videos. Default behaviour; override only for a specific, stated reason.
- **Strong**: 5-7 channels. Apply unless the user's situation clearly says otherwise.
- **Supporting**: 3-4 channels. Use as tie-breakers.
- **Minor**: 1-2 channels. Mention only when relevant.

"Resume" and "CV" mean the same job-application document here, as they do in the UK, Europe, Africa and most of Asia. The exception is the **academic CV** (Workflow E), which is a different document.

## Ground rules (always)

Honesty is a Core law (9/18 · 9/16), and the one whose failure costs the user most: offers get withdrawn after background checks, and candidates get exposed in interviews.

1. **Never fabricate or inflate** employers, job titles, levels, dates, degrees, graduation status, people managed, skills, software, languages, certifications or numbers. That includes changing the user's location when they aren't moving, white/hidden text, and fake references. If asked, say briefly why it backfires and offer the honest version (see "Not adopted" in `references/consensus-tally.md`).
2. **Numbers come from the user.** Use only figures the user gave or confirmed. A rough estimate is fine if the user can explain how they got it. Otherwise write `[number?]` and ask. Never let an AI-generated figure stand, including your own.
3. **Everything must be interview-defensible.** Keep only claims the user could talk about for two minutes.
4. **Honest framing is allowed and expected.** Translate an odd internal title into the market title for the *same* level of work. Claim decisions the user really made. Leave out short, irrelevant or bad-fit jobs (and from LinkedIn too). Describe real freelance, caregiving or study during a gap.

## Step 1: Gather the inputs before writing

1. **Target.** Ask for the full job posting. If there isn't one, get the job title, industry and seniority, and say that the result is tailored to a title rather than a posting. Also find out the country or region, since norms differ, and the career stage: teen/first job, student, new graduate, experienced, career changer, senior/executive, or academic.
2. **Raw material.** The current resume and/or LinkedIn, plus a brain dump for each role or project: the problem, what *they* did, how (tools and methods), the result, and the scale. Use the questions in `references/templates-and-examples.md` §1. Messy is fine. Facts first, wording later.
3. **Constraints.** Required format (PDF or Word), page expectations, application-form questions (location, work authorisation, years of experience), and anything the user wants left off.

If information is missing, ask for the few things that matter most (the posting and the numbers) rather than guessing. When you must proceed without them, mark every assumption.

## Step 2: Core laws (apply all seven)

### 1. Tailor to each target job, in the posting's own words (12/18 · 10/16)
- Extract the posting's must-haves (skills, tools, methods, qualifications) and note which terms repeat, because those matter most. Build the keyword map in `references/templates-and-examples.md` §2.
- **Use the exact wording** where it describes the user's real experience (10/18 · 9/16): "stakeholder management", not a synonym; "AWS", not "cloud". State it rather than relying on implication; one recruiter's version: if X implies Y, write Y anyway.
- Put the terms **inside experience bullets**, not only in the skills list.
- Tailoring means changing the title line, the summary, which bullets appear, their order and the skills order. It never means changing facts.
- **Map, don't stuff** (Supporting, 4/18 · 4/16): no hidden text, no pasted posting paragraphs, no skills the user lacks. In Jeff Su's cited data, tailored resumes got 84% more interviews, but the most keyword-heavy got 21% *fewer* than moderately tailored ones. Don't chase a 100% "ATS score".

### 2. Write achievements, not duties (12/18 · 11/16)
- Every bullet answers **"so what?"**: what changed because the user was there. A bullet that reads like the job ad ("responsible for...", "contributed to...", "involved in...") is a duty; rewrite it.
- Use a formula (9/18 · 8/16). Pick one and stay consistent:
  - **XYZ**: accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z.
  - **Action-context-result**: verb, what and where, outcome.
  - **What + how + why + where**: the skill or keyword, how it was used, the plain-English business reason, under a real role or project. This is Headless Headhunter's "qualification": a keyword alone doesn't count.
  - **Decision + outcome**: what the user chose to do differently, and what it achieved.
- No separate "Key achievements" section; every bullet should already be one.

### 3. Quantify, believably (12/18 · 11/16)
- Prefer numbers: percentages, time saved, money saved or earned (where relevant), volume, scale (users, customers, team size, budget, sites), speed, accuracy, before/after.
- Revenue isn't the only metric. Time, error rate, throughput and satisfaction all count.
- Keep figures believable for the company's size and budget, and never present the employer's scale as the user's achievement.
- A reasonable target is a number or concrete outcome in most bullets. If fewer than half have one, that's the first fix.
- *Counterpoint (Headless Headhunter, 2 videos):* vanity numbers that don't matter to the target job, or small numbers set against a big employer's scale, can hurt. Pick the metrics the target role cares about. When a number would look small, use a concrete outcome instead.

### 4. Be specific; cut generic filler (12/18 · 11/16)
- Name the tools, methods, equipment, industry, what was built or changed, and for whom.
- Delete clichés and self-praise: results-driven, proven track record, dynamic, passionate, hard-working, team player, detail-oriented, "spearheaded cross-functional initiatives". **Show the trait with evidence instead.** A soft skill belongs inside a bullet that demonstrates it, never in a list.
- Test each bullet: **could it appear on anyone's resume?** If so, rewrite it. Generic, same-shaped bullets are also the main tell of an AI-written resume. Vary sentence rhythm and keep the user's voice.
- Write for two readers: a non-specialist recruiter who must see the fit, and the hiring manager who checks depth. If two people in the user's own field couldn't follow a bullet, simplify it; if the bullet has no technical substance, add it.

### 5. Make the fit visible in a 6-20 second skim (11/18 · 10/16)
- The first look is seconds long; recruiters cite 6-8 seconds, and one reviewer gives himself 20. In that time they check the target and level, the most recent or relevant role, and one strong result.
- **Front-load:** the most relevant role and bullets first, the top 2-3 achievements at the top of each role, and the job's core requirement in the first bullet of the first entry. Put a title line matching the posting under the name.
- Bold sparingly: job titles and employers, not random phrases.

### 6. Plain, readable layout that software and people can both read (10/18 · 9/16)
- **ATS rules** (6/18 · 6/16): one column; no tables, text boxes, multi-column templates, icons, graphics, skill bars or progress circles; contact details in the body, not in the page header or footer. A parser turns the file into plain text, and an AI ranker only ever sees that text, so a parse failure ruins the ranking too.
- Standard fonts (Calibri, Arial, Helvetica, Times New Roman) at roughly 10.5-12 pt, normal margins, white space, and consistent dates, alignment and punctuation.
- No photo unless it's the local norm (e.g. parts of Europe and Asia).
- "Boring is best": the content gets the interview, not the design. Creativity belongs in a portfolio.
- *Dissent:* Adam (engVid) thinks side boxes are fine. He's outvoted; don't use them.

### 7. Honest and interview-defensible (9/18 · 9/16)
See the ground rules. Put another way, the resume is a conversation-starter, so every line must be one the user is glad to be asked about.

## Step 3: Strong principles (5-7 channels)

- **Length (7/18 · 7/16).** One page for students and early career. Two at most for roughly 5-10+ years or senior roles; a third page risks rejection. Keep everything else in a longer master resume (Supporting).
- **Standard headings (7/18 · 7/16).** Summary, Experience (or Work Experience), Education, Skills, Projects, Certifications. No creative headings ("My Journey") or vague ones ("Additional"); "Skills" rather than "Core competencies".
- **Summary (7/18 · 7/16).** Two or three lines, tailored, under a title that matches the posting:
  1. Identity, level and years.
  2. The best relevant achievement, with a number.
  3. What the user brings to this team.
  Entry-level candidates use a short objective instead: who they are, the job they want, and why they'd be good at it. *Dissent (Headless Headhunter):* skip it unless it says something the rest can't, such as relocation, a visa situation or a career change. **Resolution:** include a summary only if it's specific; never a generic one.
- **Concise bullets (7/18 · 7/16).** One or two lines each, no paragraphs, no filler words. Don't let a last line hold one or two orphaned words.
- **Contact details and proof links (7/18 · 7/16).** Name; a phone number dialable in the target country; a professional, working email; city and region (no street address). Add LinkedIn, plus a portfolio or GitHub when the field expects one, and test every link. "Phone available on request" gets resumes discarded.
- **Cut what doesn't serve the target (8/18 · 6/16).** It's a highlight reel, not an autobiography. Drop high school once at university, internships once the user has a full-time job, jobs older than about 10-15 years, unrelated jobs (unless dropping them leaves a confusing gap), and irrelevant coursework, clubs and certificates.
- **AI as assistant, not author (6/18 · 6/16).** "Great for clarity, not creation" (Microsoft's recruiter). The user supplies facts, AI tightens the wording, and the user reviews every suggestion and deletes anything untrue or generic. This applies to you too: you are the AI here.
- **Strong verbs and real ownership (6/18 · 6/16).** Start bullets with a past-tense action verb (present tense for the current role). Use no verb three or more times. Claim decisions the user really made: "led the Q3 email campaign from concept to launch", not "assisted with campaigns". Never claim direct reports who didn't exist.
- **Proofread, get a second pair of eyes, keep formatting consistent (6/18 · 6/16).** One typo can cost an interview. Read it aloud, wait a day, and ask a friend or careers service.
- **Projects for students, career changers and people with thin experience (6/18 · 6/16).** Real projects with users, results and links (code, demo, portfolio, or even a write-up doc). One deep project that people actually used beats several toy ones (Peralta); ouckah valued a range of different project types.
- **Two gates (6/18 · 6/16).** First the ATS/AI parses and ranks; then a human skims; then the hiring manager reads. Only 6% of hiring managers let AI decide (Jeff Su), so write for both gates.
- **Every listed skill needs a receipt (6/18 · 5/16).** Each skill in the Skills section should also appear in a bullet showing it used. Group hard skills into categories, ordered by what the posting emphasises. No soft-skill lists, ratings or stars.
- **File (5/18 · 5/16).** A PDF with selectable text (test it by trying to highlight the text), under about 2-2.5 MB, named like `First-Last-Resume-Company.pdf`. Send Word if the employer asks for it.
- **3-5 bullets per role (5/18 · 5/16).** Use more for the current and most relevant roles and 2-3 for older or short ones. Don't under-describe a long tenure either: five bullets for seven years looks thin.
- **Signals worth a line if relevant (5/18 · 5/16).** Awards, scholarships, recent certifications (the last ~18 months signals active learning), leadership roles, publications, teaching-assistant roles, competition and hackathon wins.

## Step 4: Supporting tie-breakers (3-4 channels)

- **Local norms (5/18 · 4/16).**
  - US/Canada: no photo, date of birth or marital status; a resume, not an academic CV.
  - Europe: "CV" usually means a resume, so follow resume rules unless the role is academic.
  - Some countries expect photos.
  - International applicants: convert the GPA scale, replace regional jargon and degree names with their local equivalents, and keep modest, passive phrasing out.
  - Don't put visa status in the resume body unless it helps (e.g. already authorised to work).
  - Moving for the job: write "Relocating to <city> by <month year>".
- **Don't undersell (4/18 · 4/16).** Employers know only what's written down. Clear detail isn't exaggeration.
- **Age signals for experienced candidates (4/18 · 3/16).** Graduation years can be dropped if they're more than 5-6 years ago. Don't headline "20+ years" unless targeting senior roles. Cover about the last 10-15 years.
- **Gaps and short stints (3/18 · 3/16).** List real freelance, consulting, caregiving, study or projects under a normal title, or leave a short stint out entirely (and from LinkedIn). Never invent an employer.
- **Master resume and saved versions (3/18 · 3/16).** Keep one long document with every role and the best bullets. For each application, select and order from it rather than rewriting from scratch, and save the version you sent.
- **Section order by stage (4/18 · 3/16).** Students and new graduates put Education first, with the expected graduation date (it shows eligibility). Experienced candidates put Experience first.
- **AI skills (4/18 · 3/16).** If the user really uses AI tools at work, show one in a bullet with an outcome; a skills-list entry isn't enough.
- **Fonts (3/18 · 3/16).** 11 pt or larger is the safe floor for body text. Never a novelty font.
- **Beyond the document (4/18 · 3/16).**
  - Answer application-form "knockout" questions accurately; the machine doesn't round up.
  - Apply early (within the first day or two, or the first ~100 applicants), on the company's own careers site where possible.
  - Track the interview rate. One recruiter treats roughly 1 interview per 7 applications as good and worse than 1 in 50 as a resume problem.

**Minor (1-2 channels), when relevant:**
- GPA only if 3.5 or higher on a 4.0 scale.
- References left off ("available on request" is optional).
- Interests only if relevant.
- Promotions shown under one employer heading, each title with its own dates.
- Upcoming internships marked "Incoming" with the team.
- Interns show teamwork and taking feedback through an example.
- LinkedIn dates, titles and employers match the resume.

## Where the creators disagree

Present these as choices with trade-offs; don't invent a consensus.

| Question | One camp | Other camp | Default |
|---|---|---|---|
| Numbers in bullets | 11 channels: quantify wherever you can | Headless Headhunter: don't "brag"; save numbers for the interview; small numbers can look weak | Quantify what the target job cares about. Use a concrete outcome when a number would mislead or look small. |
| Summary at the top | 7 channels: short tailored summary or objective | Headless Headhunter: only for relocation, visa or career change | Include only if specific; never generic. |
| Skills block | Heather Austin: 9-12 posting skills in one grouped line near the top | Joshua Fluke: no "core competencies"; just a Skills section of tools. Headless Headhunter: skills lists "don't count" | Short grouped Skills section of hard skills, each proven in a bullet; no soft-skill lists. |
| Tailor per job or per title | Most: tailor to each posting | Headless Headhunter: tailor per job *title*, then apply fast and widely | Master resume, then a base version per role type, then quick edits per posting for the jobs that matter. |
| Education or experience first | Jeff Su: experience first unless a fresh graduate | Headless Headhunter: a relevant degree goes on top even later | Students and new grads: Education first. Others: Experience first. |
| Coursework | Peralta, ouckah, Resume Genius: students keep the 4-8 most relevant | Headless Headhunter: never relevant | Students only, and only if it fills a real gap in relevant experience. |
| Projects | Peralta and co-reviewer: one deep project with real users | ouckah: a range of projects shows breadth | Lead with the deepest, most used project; add range only if there's room. |
| Dates | Adam: dates on everything | Farah Sharghi, Fluke, Headless Headhunter: experienced people can drop graduation years | Always give employment dates (month/year). Graduation year: show it if recent, optional after ~5-6 years. |
| Design | Everyone else: one column, no boxes | Adam: side boxes are fine | One column. |

## Workflows

### A. Write a resume from scratch, or build the master version
1. Run Step 1 and collect a brain dump for every role, project and activity.
2. Choose the layout for the career stage (`references/templates-and-examples.md` §3).
3. Draft bullets with Core laws 2-4, marking unknown numbers `[number?]`.
4. Write the title line and summary last, once the strongest results are known.
5. Fit to length by cutting the least relevant items (Strong: cut for relevance), not by shrinking the font.
6. Return the draft, the list of `[number?]` questions, and the pre-send checklist (§9).

### B. Tailor to a specific posting
1. Build the keyword map (§2) and flag honest gaps to the user.
2. Re-title the resume to match the posting; rewrite the summary for this job.
3. For each role, choose and order bullets by relevance to this posting, and mirror its terms where true.
4. Reorder the Skills categories. Check that each must-have appears in a bullet, and in the first bullet of the first role where possible.
5. Report what changed and which must-haves are still uncovered, and why.

### C. Review or rate a resume ("rate my resume", "why no interviews?")
1. Do the 20-second skim test first and say what a recruiter would take away.
2. Score the seven Core laws 0-2 using the scorecard (§8) and list failed Strong checks.
3. Give the top 3-5 problems in order of impact, each tied to its principle and weight.
4. Rewrite 2-3 representative bullets to show the fix.
5. If the user reports an interview rate (e.g. 2 interviews from 100 applications), relate it to the "beyond the document" points too; the resume isn't always the only problem.

### D. Rewrite bullets
For each bullet: name what's wrong (duty, vague, generic, no result, no keyword, too long). Rewrite it with one formula. List what you need the user to confirm. Never add a fact or number the user didn't give.

### E. Special cases
- **Teen or first job:** one page with an objective, education, real informal experience (babysitting, volunteering, clubs, sport, family business, school projects) and concrete skills; GPA only if strong; a professional email address.
- **Student or new graduate:** Education first (expected date, strong GPA, scholarships), then internships, research and TA roles, then projects with results and links, then relevant leadership. One page.
- **Career changer:** a summary that names the new target; transferable skills shown in bullets, using the new field's vocabulary; projects or certifications in the new field; old-field jargon removed. An unrelated degree goes at the bottom.
- **Experienced or senior:** Experience first, the last 10-15 years, two pages at most, graduation year optional; show scope (budget, team, revenue, scale) without inflating it.
- **Gap or short stints:** apply the ground rules; describe real activity plainly, keep a brief explanation ready for the interview, and stay consistent with LinkedIn.
- **Academic CV:** for academic jobs, PhD or graduate-school applications, fellowships and medical residencies, use the full-record structure in `references/templates-and-examples.md` §7. There's no page limit, but only what's relevant.

## Output format

When writing or rewriting a resume, return:

```
<the resume in plain text / Markdown, single column, using the layout from references §3>

Open questions: <each [number?] or unclear fact, as a short question>
Tailoring notes: <posting must-haves covered; honest gaps not covered>
Before sending: <the 3-5 checklist items most likely to be missed for this user>
```

When reviewing:

```
Skim verdict: <what a recruiter sees in 20 seconds>
Score: <x/14 on the Core laws, one line per law>
Top fixes: <3-5 bullets, each tied to a principle and its weight, e.g. "Achievements, not duties (Core, 12/18)">
Example rewrites: <2-3 before → after>
Facts to confirm: <anything assumed>
```

Keep feedback direct and specific. The creators agree that vague praise doesn't help, and none of them ever recommends lying.

## Caveats

- **Sample skew.** 8 of the 18 videos are about software, data or tech resumes, and most advice is US-centric. Generalise tech specifics to "the tools and methods of the field", and check local norms.
- **Sponsorship and tools.** Several videos promote a tool (Enhancv, PDFelement, the creators' own builders, prompt packs). Read "use tool X" as "use any resume tool or AI, then verify the output".
- **Statistics are cited, not verified.** "75% rejected by ATS" and "6-7 seconds" are widely repeated industry claims. Don't put them in the user's documents or present them as precise.
- **Two of the 20 picks are missing**, and the window is a single 6-month period of AI-heavy hiring. See `references/sources.md`.

## Refreshing the research

The scripts repeat the selection. YouTube blocks automated transcript downloads from servers and from automated browsers, so the transcripts themselves are saved by hand. Steps for a Windows PC are in `RUN-ON-WINDOWS.md`.

1. `python3 scripts/fetch_cv_transcripts.py candidates <out>` runs the searches and metadata checks (yt-dlp), or reuse `research/candidates.json`. Review the list and remove off-topic videos.
2. `node scripts/fetch_transcripts_browser.cjs --list` (after `npm ci`) checks dates and languages in a browser and writes `out/save-these-20.html`. That page has the 20 links and a "Save transcript" favourite.
3. Save each transcript from YouTube's transcript panel in a normal browser.
4. Re-read the transcripts, re-code the themes in `references/consensus-tally.md`, and update the weights here.

Transcripts are git-ignored and never committed, because they are the creators' content.
